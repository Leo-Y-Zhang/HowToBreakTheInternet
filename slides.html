RSA: a padlock anyone can close, only you can open

1977: Rivest, Shamir, Adleman (MIT).  Actually 1973: Clifford, GCHQ, Cheltenham. Top secret until 1997.

The padlock = the big number N.
Your public key. Give copies to everyone. Anyone can snap it shut on a message.

The key = the two primes.
Your private key. Only you have them. Know the primes → open the padlock.

Don't know them? Un-multiply 617 digits.

What a quantum computer is not

“A computer that tries every answer at once.”

If that were true it could solve everything instantly. It can't.
It's brilliant at a few very specific jobs. Factoring happens to be one.

Before you look: a blend. When you look: it picks. The dial sets the odds.  A spinning coin is not a computer.

The secret ingredient: signs

Every possibility inside the machine is a wave. Peak on peak: bigger. Peak on trough: nothing.

The one thing to remember

A quantum computer is not a machine that tries every answer at once.

It's a machine for choreographing interference so the wrong answers cancel out.

Designing the choreography is the algorithm. For most problems, nobody knows one.
For factoring, Peter Shor found one.

Why it's taken thirty years

  • The waves are fragile: heat, light, a passing lorry scrambles them
  • So: colder than outer space, in a vacuum, shielded
  • …and the qubits still make mistakes constantly
  • Fix: gang up ~1,000 noisy physical qubits → 1 reliable “logical” qubit

Remember that thousand. It matters later.

Shor's trick

Don't factor.
Find the rhythm.

Remainders · powers · highest common factor. All GCSE.

Clock arithmetic

17 mod 15 = 2

49 mod 15 = 4

“mod” means: divide, keep the remainder. Wrap around like a clock: 15 o'clock is 3 o'clock.

Powers of 7, wrapped around 15

a   N same colour = same remainder

The rhythm hands you the factors

Period 4 → halve it → 2

72 = 49 → look either side: 48 and 50

HCF(48, 15) = 3

HCF(50, 15) = 5

15 = 3 × 5.  We never divided 15 by anything.

Why: 7⁴ − 1 is a multiple of 15, and 7⁴ − 1 = (7² − 1)(7² + 1). Difference of two squares.

So: factoring = finding the period

For the 617-digit number, the rhythm repeats after a number of steps that is itself hundreds of digits long.

Walking along it step by step: longer than the universe has existed.
Back where we started.

Appendix · toy RSA with real numbers

Secret primes: p = 61, q = 53

Padlock (public): N = 61 × 53 = 3233, e = 17

Key (private): d = 2753  (needs (p−1)(q−1) = 3120)

Lock the number 65: 6517 mod 3233 = 2790

Unlock: 27902753 mod 3233 = 65

Factor 3233 → recompute d → read everything.

Real keys use 300-digit primes. Same maths, just bigger. Demo Code/toy_rsa.py runs this.

Appendix · why the rhythm gives the factors

ar ≡ 1 (mod N)  →  ar − 1 is a multiple of N

ar − 1 = (ar/2 − 1)(ar/2 + 1)  difference of two squares

So N's prime factors are shared between the two brackets…

…and HCF(ar/2 − 1, N) and HCF(ar/2 + 1, N) pick them out.

Fails if r is odd (can't halve) or ar/2 ≡ −1 (one bracket is a multiple of N). Random a: works more than half the time. Shor proved it.

Appendix · what else breaks?

ThingQuantum verdict
RSA, Diffie–Hellman, elliptic curves (TLS, Bitcoin, signatures)Broken by Shor. Bitcoin needs fewer qubits than RSA-2048.
AES, ChaCha (the actual message encryption)Fine. Grover halves the effective key length; AES-256 stays strong.
Password hashesFine. Use a long password; quantum doesn't guess better.
New lattice-based padlocks (ML-KEM, ML-DSA)No known quantum attack. It's a bet, like RSA was, but a well-tested one.

Appendix · how to spot a fake “quantum breaks RSA” headline

  • Number smaller than about 50 bits? A laptop does that in a millisecond.
  • “Annealer” or “D-Wave”? Not Shor's algorithm; doesn't scale.
  • “Compiled” circuit, or they knew the answer first? Not a real factoring.
  • No error correction mentioned? It can't be anywhere near RSA-2048.
  • Sober check: Scott Aaronson's blog will have already explained why it's nothing.

Honest record as of this talk: 21. If that changes, it will be enormous news from Google, IBM or Quantinuum, with a paper.
